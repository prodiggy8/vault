## Chapter 10

- Oracle is cool again: E-Business Suite
- Internet fever: Oracle one of big 4 for infrastructure (storage from EMC, Cisco routers, Sun servers)
- Were doing well against SAP and Peoplesoft in the ERP business
- JVM built-in

**11i**

- tight integration between back-office (ERP) and front-office (CRM) applications
- completeness, with about 140 modules and no manual gaps
- one common data model shared by all of them

More than 4,000 developers worked on it, and Ellison called it the largest software product ever built.

- Customers sit on their wallets if there’s something new coming
- Europe was adapting to Euro and planning to do it together with 11i

**What went wrong**

- Delayed
- Put a brand-new, untested order management module into the first release together with the old one so customers choose. Impossible, so they did the new one.

- Oracle also breaks a basic rule by shipping before running the software itself, so customers become the guinea pigs. The beta testing is also flawed, because it ran on Oracle's own machines and hid the upgrade problems.



After launch, complaints pile up. The rumor of "five thousand patches" sticks to Oracle for most of a year. When Oracle finally moves itself onto 11i over New Year's, its order system is down for two weeks. A fixed release (11.5.3) arrives in February 2001, but the bad press continues. A feud with the Oracle Applications Users Group (who had the right to stage conferences), which gives the press a steady supply of negative quotes, adds to it.
- Talk to users, no logos vs advertise
- OAUG continued to run conferences without support, Oracle ran their own
- “Arrogant” company

Several things then hit at once:

- Wall Street analysts turn gloomy as the dot-coms collapse.
- Ellison's AppsWorld speech in Paris attacks systems integrators, many of whom are the event's sponsors, and he later seems to say customers must buy the whole suite.
	- Better to buy full product than to customize
	- “Evil folk ruining their softwares”
- Oracle issues a profit warning on March 1, and the stock falls 21%.
	- 2 cents below, but database sales stagnant to negative
- Class-action suits follow over Ellison's share sales.
	- Lies over EBS and 11i
- Applications growth comes in at about 25%, against the 75% forecast in February.

Wohl admits the release should have been held back three or four months. Ellison compares the pain to the Version 6 database crisis and expects the gain to be as large. The chapter ends with Oracle rattled but convinced the suite strategy is right, and with rivals now copying it.

“Despite everything, at least the idea of the integrated suite was beginning to win acceptance, not just from customers but even from rivals, like SAP and PeopleSoft.”

## Chapter 11

This chapter follows Ellison through one day in April 2001. Announce change of strategy about 11i.

- Recognizes too aggressive to system integrators

- Suite sold as "business flows" such as campaign-to-cash that can be installed one at a time. This corrects the impression that customers must buy everything at once. It comes with a guarantee of "CRM in ninety days" at a fixed price. 

Once decision on promotion -> software filters leads and sends e-mails in minutes. No sales go to telesales, sales is automated.

One feature companies will die – he says.

“Ellison starts off by saying that the software industry’s preoccupation with offering separate applications for what are really continuous processes has made “the graceful flow of information” hard to achieve.”

- **The product meeting.** In the weekly meeting, he discovers that one flow due to be announced the next day, a sales-compensation package, doesn't work. He has it pulled and insists nothing should be released until it works at Oracle.

- **The lost quarter**

Ellison rejects the idea that a couple of big deals fell through at the last minute. He says it was dozens. Some slipped, and others shrank, for example from about $6 million to $2 million. The pattern was that buyers stopped purchasing for their needs over the next couple of years and bought only what they needed for the next three months. He also describes customers already running Oracle software looking for ways to avoid paying. One large wireless company disputed a bill of $15 to $20 million as a negotiating tactic, and Oracle refused to cut it.

Dot-com die-out had changed how companies felt about the Internet. Established firms no longer feared "stealth competitors" like Amazon, so they felt less urgency to move processes online.

- **Rivals**

_Siebel._ Siebel was outselling Oracle in CRM about four to one. Its 2001 revenue passed $2 billion, net earnings were $255 million, and it held over $1 billion in cash. The author recalls meeting Tom Siebel in 1999, when Siebel handed him a note titled "The six lies of Larry Ellison" to pass on. Siebel defines his company partly against Ellison's style, rejecting the cult of the software CEO. Part of that is requiring suits and ties as a sign of respect to customers.

He says the software is still client/server and the Internet version isn't due until the fall. He says Siebel hit its last quarter with software swaps and an IBM deal and can't repeat that. His prediction is that Siebel will crash. A footnote in the book adds "And crash he did."

Siebel bought companies such as Scopus to fill gaps. Ellison refused, because he wanted the architectural integrity of a suite built on one data model.

_Acquisitions._ Ellison says Oracle has never paid more than about $125 million for an acquisition. He says Oracle would look at Clarify, the call-center specialist, but wouldn't buy it because it can't be integrated.

His admitted mistake is PeopleSoft. In 2000, with its stock depressed, Oracle could have bought it for two or three percent of its own shares. His executives thought a first big acquisition was too risky while Oracle's own re-engineering consumed more than all its management capacity. Ellison also doubted PeopleSoft was a real suite company, since about 65% of its business was HR.

_SAP._ Ellison credits SAP with inventing the integrated ERP suite, with strong installed-base and German-market positions, and with being an engineering-driven company like Oracle. Both have engineers as CEOs, and he says SAP's engineers love Oracle's database.

- **The CEO roundtable**

He gives his usual story of Oracle's own chaos, the failure of IT to deliver, and the benefits of an integrated suite on one database. The funny, confessional tone is designed to get guests to share their own horror stories.

He says Brunswick spent $250 million on systems in recent years with almost nothing to show for it, calling the result a hodgepodge of PeopleSoft, i2 and Oracle. He adds that Oracle's own consultants steered his IT staff toward other vendors when they raised deficiencies, and that he won't spend another $200 million to fix it.

His advice is then to cut the budget. Stop some integration projects, redirect part of the money to put 11i into one business unit, and expand if it proves itself.

Replacing 97 local email systems with one global system, and 2,000 Windows file servers with one global file-sharing system, were quick wins. He claims they cut costs 50 to 75% in about ninety days, saved more than the $250 million, and helped halve the IT budget.


Kovacevich asks whether adopting the software's practices erodes what makes a company unique. Oracle's Steve Perkins answers that Oracle's apps are flexible, unlike SAP's. -> **flexibility**


## Chapter 12

This chapter is a case study of a GE Power Systems turbine plant at Veresegyház, Hungary. It is a roughly $100 million factory built at record speed and used by Jack Welch as a test of whether a unit could become a full e-business. 

GE was building a new factory in Hungary and needed new business software running by a deadline. About a month in, the local IT firm helping with the setup was falling behind, so Oracle sent in a large team of consultants to rescue it.

The plant manager, Craig Kipp, then sat through a practice run of the software, where staff pretended to run the business on it. He saw that the people deciding how his factory would operate were junior employees and Oracle consultants, not him or his senior managers.

The practice run also showed a real problem. The software had been tweaked to track costs in a very detailed way, because GE's finance team had asked for that. In the simulation, workers collected parts for the wrong job. Kipp realized that if this went live, the factory floor would be thrown into chaos.

That night he called a meeting in a hallway and told everyone to stop making it so complicated. The tweaks had piled up because nobody had asked him what he actually needed.

Kipp's diagnosis is that consultants kept asking customers what else they would like the software to do, which turned a product that fit about 80% of the need into a customization project. He thinks the install could have taken three months instead of five without that. The fix was for senior managers to commit as much as half their working day to making the process decisions. He later tells other GE managers to take the software as configured and wait for new features in later releases. When Oracle offered a requested feature in six months, he accepted the wait rather than ask for custom code.

His lesson for Oracle is that its consultants should have told the customer no. Ellison's footnotes agree. Oracle's consultants are now trained to refuse code changes, because Oracle makes more money from software than from consulting. He also argues that you should automate whole end-to-end flows, not single departments.

The three chapters form one arc. Oracle overreaches with 11i, takes a heavy hit in 2001, and then rebuilds its pitch around a simple idea: don't modify the software, start small, and aim for 80% fast.
