# Equations of Lines and Planes in Space

Two nonzero vectors $u$ and $v$ are parallel if and only if $u=kv$ for some scalar $k$.
$$
\begin{align}
\langle x, y, z \rangle = \langle x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0} \rangle + t\langle a, b, c \rangle \\
\end{align}
$$
We can also define a set of equations tied to parameter $t$:
$$
\begin{align}
x = x_{0}+ta && y = y_{0} + tb && z = z_{0} + tc
\end{align}
$$
If $a$, $b$, $c$ are nonzero, then $L$ can be described by the symmetric equations of the line:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{x-x_{0}}{a}=t && \frac{y-y_{0}}{b}=t && \frac{z-z_{0}}{c} = t
\end{align}
$$
The parametric representations are **not unique**. Using a different parallel vector $\langle a, b, c \rangle$ or different point $(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})$ leads to a different way of writing the same line.

We can also **limit** the values of $t$ if we don’t want an infinite line. Suppose we want just a line segment from $P$ to $Q$:
$$r=P + t(\vec{PQ})=P+t(Q-P)$$
Or the book can do a wall of algebra to get to a standard form seem in various examples and applications:
$$r=(1-t)P + tQ$$
We can from both equations clearly see that $0 \leq t \leq 1$ to represent the line segment (we find that out by setting the equation to $P, Q$ and solving for $t$).

# Distance between a point and a line

Let $L$ be a line and $M$ be a point not in the line. We define the distance as the length of the line $\overline{MP}$ where $P$ is a point on the line and $\overline{MP}$ is perpendicular to $L$.

We don’t know exactly how to pick an optimal $P$ such that $\overline{MP}$ is perpendicular so we pick $P$ at random and use the parallelogram method to find $d$.

Given $P$ and $v$, the direction vector for $L$, we know the area of the parallelogram using cross product but we also know the area is equal to base times height, so:
$$
|\overline{PM} \times v| = |v| d
$$
Hence:
$$d=\frac{|\overline{PM} \times v|}{|v|}$$
## Lines

In 2D, either two lines are the same, parallel, or intersect at a point. In 3D they might be skewed so that none of the above applies. To classify them we need two things:
1. Are the parallel?
	If they are then $u=kv$ and $u \cdot v = 1 \text{ or } u \cdot v = -1$.
2. Do they share a point?
	Set the parametric equations equal to each other and find out. It will form a system of equations with two unknowns.

## Planes

- Given 3 points that do not lie all in the same line, there is a unique plane that passes through these points.
- Given two distinct, intersecting lines, there is exactly one plane containing both.

Let $n=\langle a, b, c \rangle$ be a vector and $P=(x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})$ be a point. Then the set of all points $Q=(x, y, z)$ such that $Q$ is orthogonal to $n$ forms a plane. We say that $n$ is a **normal vector** and as such $n \cdot \vec{PQ} = 0$.

**Distance**

For point $P$, we find a point $R$ in the plane such that $\vec{RP}$ is orthogonal to the plane.

Given a **known point in the plane** $Q$ then the projection of $Q$ onto $n$ allows us to find $\vec{RP}$:
$$d=|\operatorname{proj}_{n}\vec{QP}|=\frac{|\vec{QP} \cdot n|}{|n|}$$
